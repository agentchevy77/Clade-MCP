## Core concepts
- Greek: partial derivative of an option's model value V(S, t, sigma, r, q) with respect to one input, holding the others fixed. Greeks are model outputs (here Black-Scholes-Merton, BSM) and only as good as the model and the inputs; the market quotes prices, and Greeks are inferred by inverting to implied vol (IV) and differentiating.
- Delta (dV/dS): hedge ratio in units of underlying per option. Call delta in (0, e^(-qT)); put delta in (-e^(-qT), 0); put delta = call delta - e^(-qT) by put-call parity. BSM call delta = e^(-qT) N(d1).
- Delta as "probability": N(d2) is the risk-neutral probability the call finishes ITM; N(d1) = delta is the probability under the stock (share) numeraire. Since d1 = d2 + sigma*sqrt(T), N(d1) > N(d2) always for calls; the absolute gap is largest ATM and grows with sigma*sqrt(T) (approx 0.4*sigma*sqrt(T) at the money) and is small for deep-OTM short-dated options (both -> 0). Measured as a ratio, however, delta/N(d2) grows away from the money and with sigma*sqrt(T): for 1y 20%-vol calls (r = 4%) it is 1.14 ATM, 1.22 at K = 110, 1.30 at K = 120 and 1.38 at K = 130, so "a 16-delta LEAP has a 16% chance of finishing ITM" overstates the risk-neutral probability by about 40%. Real-world probability also differs via drift and the market's skewed distribution; use N(d2) or the market-implied digital price for probability statements.
- Gamma (d2V/dS2 = dDelta/dS): convexity; same for call and put at a strike; always >= 0 for vanilla long options. Long gamma = position profits from large moves in either direction (after delta hedging); short gamma = loses.
- Theta (dV/dt, usually quoted per day): time decay; the "rent" paid for gamma. Negative for long vanillas except deep-ITM European puts when r > 0 (and deep-ITM calls when q is large), where the +r*K*e^(-rT)*N(-d2) term dominates and theta is positive (S = 100, K = 150, 1y, r = 5%, 20% vol: Theta_put = +5.9/yr); American deep-ITM puts are exercised early precisely to avoid the European contract's negative carry. Theta and gamma are tied by the BSM PDE (see Formulas).
- Vega (dV/dsigma, quoted per 1 vol point = 0.01): same for call and put. Not a true Greek letter; sometimes "kappa".
- Rho (dV/dr): rate sensitivity; positive for calls, negative for puts on stock. For options on futures priced with Black-76 and stock-style (up-front) premium, rho = -T*V holding F fixed (only the discount factor depends on r); for genuinely futures-style (margined-premium) options rho = 0. Epsilon / psi (dV/dq): dividend-yield sensitivity; negative for calls, positive for puts.
- Vanna (d2V/dS dsigma = dDelta/dsigma = dVega/dS): how delta moves when IV moves and how vega moves when spot moves. Zero near d2 = 0 (roughly ATM-forward), positive for OTM calls / ITM puts (high strikes), negative for low strikes.
- Volga / vomma (d2V/dsigma2 = dVega/dsigma): vol convexity. Near zero ATM (d1*d2 ~ 0), positive and largest in the wings, peaking roughly 1.4 standard deviations (|d1| ~ sqrt(2)) from the forward, i.e. around the 8-10 delta strikes (K ~ 109 for the 30-day example below, K ~ 134 for 1y at 20% vol). Long wings = long volga = benefit from IV changes in either direction.
- Charm (dDelta/dt = dTheta/dS): "delta bleed" - how delta drifts as calendar time passes with spot unchanged; large for OTM/ITM near expiry. Quoted here as d/dt (see Formulas for the d/dtau convention).
- Veta (vega decay), Color (gamma decay/growth with time), Speed (dGamma/dS), Zomma (dGamma/dsigma), Ultima (dVolga/dsigma): second/third order. Veta and color are given below as d/dtau (tau = time to expiry), the convention of most formula collections; d/dt = -d/dtau.
- Dollar Greeks: Greeks scaled into P&L units per contract or per book: delta dollars (Delta*S*multiplier), gamma per 1% move (change in delta dollars for a 1% spot move), vega per vol point, theta per day.
- Moneyness: spot/strike relationship; "ATM" in practice usually means ATM-forward (K ~ F = S*e^((r-q)T)) or the 50-delta strike.
- Delta hedging: holding -Delta units of underlying against a long option (or +Delta against a short) so first-order spot risk is zero. Gamma scalping: re-hedging a long-gamma position after moves to lock in realized variance; its expected profit equals realized minus implied variance weighted by dollar gamma.
- Hedge instrument: index options are delta-hedged with futures (ES/SPX futures) or the ETF; the futures hedge ratio is Delta_F = Delta_S*e^(-(r-q)*T_F) with T_F the time to the futures' delivery, because dF/dS = e^((r-q)*T_F). Using the share-equivalent delta in futures over-hedges by the carry factor: ~0.2% per month and ~2.7% per year at r = 4%, q = 1.3% (noise for a 30-day option, ~4% on an 18-month LEAP). Single-stock options are hedged with shares; the hedge carries borrow cost and dividend exposure, and an ex-dividend drop the forward already priced is not a "move" for gamma purposes. Mis-stating the dividend yield or discrete dividend schedule shifts the forward and therefore every Greek through d1 (for a 1y ATM call the epsilon of -0.62 per 1% of yield is the size of that exposure); futures hedges carry basis/roll risk instead.
- Daily P&L explain: the Taylor-expansion attribution of marked P&L to Greeks (delta, gamma, theta, vega, vanna, volga, rho, carry) plus an "unexplained" residual; the standard desk reconciliation tool (see Formulas).
- Break-even move: the daily move size at which gamma P&L equals theta loss; for an ATM option at implied vol sigma_i it is approximately S*sigma_i/sqrt(252) (the "IV/16 rule"). Book break-even vol sigma_be = sqrt(-2*Theta/(Gamma*S^2)) is the portfolio analogue (see Formulas).
- Discrete-hedging error: the random P&L arising because hedges are rebalanced at finite intervals; its mean is ~0 but standard deviation scales as 1/sqrt(N) for N rehedges.
- Jump / gap risk: the hedged P&L across a discontinuous move (overnight gap, earnings, macro print), equal to the full convexity term V(S(1+J)) - V(S) - Delta*S*J realized at once; unlike diffusion hedging error it is not reduced by rehedging more often.
- Vol regimes (Derman 1999): sticky strike (each strike's IV fixed when spot moves), sticky delta / sticky moneyness (IV fixed as a function of K/S or delta; the smile rides with spot), sticky local vol / implied tree (smile moves opposite to spot). Each implies a different "true" delta for a fixed-strike option.
- Skew-adjusted delta / shadow delta: delta_BS + vega * (dsigma_K/dS), i.e. BSM delta corrected for the expected change in the option's own IV when spot moves. "Shadow gamma/delta" is Taleb's (1997) term for Greeks computed while letting other parameters (vol, skew) co-move with spot. Minimum-variance (MV) delta (Hull and White 2017) is the empirically estimated version; local-vol, SABR (Bartlett) and stochastic-vol deltas are model-implied versions (see Mechanisms).
- Weighted vega / vega buckets: vega aggregated by maturity bucket and scaled by the empirical sensitivity of each maturity's IV to a reference maturity's IV (vol beta), because short-dated IV moves more than long-dated IV.
- American-option Greeks: US single-stock and ETF options are American; their Greeks come from a lattice or finite-difference grid, not closed forms. The early-exercise boundary makes deep-ITM put delta -1 and theta discontinuous there, and BSM closed forms are biased for deep-ITM puts (r > 0) and for calls just before an ex-dividend date; assignment risk on short calls before ex-date and on deep-ITM short puts is the practical rho/epsilon exposure.
- Pin risk: uncertainty at expiration about whether a short option near the strike will be assigned, leaving the writer with an unknown underlying position after the close; and the related tendency of spot to "pin" to strikes with large open interest.
- 0DTE: options expiring the same day; SPX (SPXW) lists expirations every trading day (Monday/Wednesday/Friday weeklies came first; Tuesday and Thursday expirations were added in 2022 [exact launch dates uncertain; verify on Cboe]), so 0DTE and pinning studies from before 2022 describe a different market. Greeks for 0DTE are extreme and unstable (see Mechanisms).

## Mechanisms
- Why theta pays for gamma: for a delta-hedged position, the BSM PDE gives Theta + 0.5*sigma^2*S^2*Gamma + (r-q)*S*Delta = r*V. With zero rates/dividends, Theta = -0.5*sigma^2*S^2*Gamma: expected gamma profit over dt at implied vol (0.5*Gamma*S^2*sigma_i^2*dt) exactly offsets theta. The hedged position therefore has zero expected P&L if realized variance equals implied variance, positive if realized > implied (long gamma), negative otherwise.
- Why gamma and vega are proportional within one maturity: BSM vega = Gamma*S^2*sigma*T. With one vol per expiry a single-expiry book cannot be gamma-neutral without being vega-neutral; with a skew the ratio S^2*sigma_K*T varies by strike, so the two decouple slightly even within an expiry and materially only across maturities (short-dated = gamma-heavy per unit vega; long-dated = vega-heavy per unit gamma). This is why a long calendar (short front / long back, same strike) is long raw vega and short gamma, and the reverse calendar the opposite. The raw-vega sign can flip once vol beta is applied: in the worked example the 1m and 3m ATM vegas are 11.4 and 19.6, so a raw-vega-neutral long calendar (short 1.72 front per 1 back) is net short ~14 of weighted vega per 1.00 of 3m vol under the sqrt(T_ref/T) weighting, because the front leg's IV moves ~1.7x the back leg's. Always read calendars in weighted vega.
- Why gamma and theta concentrate ATM and explode near expiry: Gamma = phi(d1)/(S*sigma*sqrt(T)). phi(d1) peaks at d1 = 0 (ATM-forward) and the 1/sqrt(T) factor diverges; ATM theta = -S*sigma*phi(d1)/(2*sqrt(T)) diverges the same way. For an OTM option, phi(d1) collapses to zero as T -> 0 faster than 1/sqrt(T) grows, so OTM gamma/theta rise, peak at an intermediate maturity, and then vanish. Hence "theta accelerates into expiry" is true for ATM (and near-ATM) options only; OTM/ITM options lose most of their extrinsic value earlier and their theta decays toward zero in the last weeks. ATM option value ~ 0.4*S*sigma*sqrt(T) means ATM decay per day ~ value/(2*days remaining): accelerating.
- Why vega is largest ATM and for longer maturities: vega = S*phi(d1)*sqrt(T): peaks at d1 = 0 and scales as sqrt(T). Measured in strike space, vega spreads out to the wings as sigma or T rises (the phi(d1) hump widens). Vega per vol point of a 1y ATM is ~3.3x that of a 1m ATM (sqrt(12) = 3.46, slightly lower with positive rates).
- Why higher IV lowers ATM gamma but raises ATM theta: Gamma ~ 1/sigma, Theta ~ sigma at the money. A high-vol regime distributes gamma over a wider strike range; a low-vol regime concentrates it (and the hedging flows) tightly around spot.
- Why short-dated IV moves more than long-dated IV (the basis of weighted vega): vol mean-reverts. In a one-factor mean-reverting variance model with speed kappa, the beta of T-maturity implied variance to a shock in instantaneous variance is (1 - e^(-kappa*T))/(kappa*T): ~1 for T << 1/kappa and decaying toward 0 for T >> 1/kappa (kappa = 2: 0.92 at 1m, 0.79 at 3m, 0.43 at 1y). The desk rule sqrt(T_ref/T) is a one-parameter approximation of this curve. It fails when the shock is to the long-run level rather than to spot variance (regime change, crisis): the whole curve shifts and back-month vega is under-weighted (Bergomi 2016 on the term structure of vol-of-vol).
- Delta-hedging P&L decomposition (Carr and Madan 1998; Ahmad and Wilmott 2005; Bergomi 2016 ch.1): for a long option marked and hedged at implied vol sigma_i, over a small interval the self-financing hedged P&L is dP&L = 0.5*Gamma_i*S^2*[(dS/S)^2 - sigma_i^2*dt] (plus financing terms that cancel when the position is funded at r). Taking expectations with realized vol sigma_r gives 0.5*Gamma_i*S^2*(sigma_r^2 - sigma_i^2)*dt. Integrated to expiry, total P&L = integral of 0.5*e^{r(T-t)}*Gamma_i(S_t,t)*S_t^2*(sigma_r^2(t) - sigma_i^2) dt: a dollar-gamma-weighted average of (realized - implied) variance along the path. Because Gamma_i depends on where spot is relative to strike, the P&L is path-dependent: the same realized vol pays very differently depending on whether the big moves occur while spot is near the strike (high gamma) or far away (low gamma). The path dependence is removed by holding options in proportion to 1/K^2 across strikes (the log contract / variance swap, which has constant dollar gamma); see Relationships.
- Which realized vol? The (dS/S)^2 in the formula is the return over the rehedge interval. A daily hedger's gamma P&L is governed by close-to-close variance (overnight gap included); a hedger who also rebalances at the open separates overnight from intraday variance; a continuous hedger captures total quadratic variation. Compare implied vol to realized vol sampled at your hedging frequency, not to a high-frequency estimator. Empirically most of the short-premium return is earned overnight: Muravyev and Ni (2020) find delta-hedged SPX option returns average about -1% per day close-to-open and +0.3% open-to-close.
- P&L attribution when implied vol moves: the decomposition above holds sigma_i fixed. With IV moving, the daily explain adds Vega*dsigma + 0.5*Volga*dsigma^2 + Vanna*dS*dsigma (+ rho, carry, higher order; formula and worked line under Formulas). This is where vanna and volga enter real P&L and why "realized vs implied" alone misses mark-to-market swings: a short-dated short-gamma book that is also short vanna loses twice in a sell-off (spot down, IV up). Use sticky-strike dsigma (the change in each strike's own IV) so the vega term and the delta term do not double-count the skew. A persistent "unexplained" residual signals a stale vol mark, a wrong dividend/rate, or discrete-hedging noise; for short-dated positions the second-order terms routinely dominate the first-order ones.
- Hedging at implied vs at "actual" vol (Ahmad and Wilmott 2005): if you knew the true future vol sigma_a and hedged with the BSM delta at sigma_a, the terminal P&L would be deterministic, V(sigma_a) - V(sigma_i), but the mark-to-market path would be noisy and can run far below zero before converging to the known terminal profit, so a trader with margin or drawdown limits can be forced out of a correct trade. Hedging at sigma_i instead makes the running P&L accrue monotonically in expectation (each day's gamma-vs-theta) but makes the terminal total path-dependent. Practitioners hedge at the market's IV (or an MV-adjusted delta) because sigma_a is unknown, marks are at IV, and the vol view is better expressed through size.
- Robustness of BSM (El Karoui, Jeanblanc-Picque and Shreve 1998): if the payoff is convex (Gamma >= 0 everywhere, true for vanillas and path-independent convex payoffs) and the hedging vol sigma_h dominates the true (possibly stochastic) vol along every path, then the BSM delta hedge at sigma_h super-replicates: terminal hedge value >= payoff. Conversely a short-vanilla position hedged at a vol below realized under-replicates. The result fails for non-convex payoffs (barriers, digitals, spreads) where gamma changes sign. Avellaneda, Levy and Paras (1995) generalize to a worst-case vol band (uncertain volatility model).
- Discrete hedging error (Boyle and Emanuel 1980; Kamal 1998): over one rehedge interval the hedged P&L is 0.5*Gamma*S^2*sigma^2*dt*(Z^2 - 1) with Z ~ N(0,1), i.e. a shifted chi-square(1): skewed, mean zero, variance 2*(0.5*Gamma*S^2*sigma^2*dt)^2. Summing N independent intervals with constant gamma gives std(P&L) ~ sigma*Vega/sqrt(2N). Kamal (1998, Goldman Sachs QS Research Notes) gives the approximation std(P&L) ~ sqrt(pi/4)*sigma*Vega/sqrt(N) for near-ATM options (stated validity: T < 1 year, r < 10%, strikes within ~0.25 put-delta of spot); the prefactor 0.886 "can be taken to be one for a rough estimate", and the note calls eq. (1) an approximation of a more complicated formula rather than an exact result. Since sigma*Vega ~ ATM premium, std/premium ~ sqrt(pi/(4N)): for a one-month ATM put at 20% vol (S = K = 100, r = 5%) the formula gives 19.3% of premium for N = 21 (daily) and 9.7% for N = 84, while the note's 50,000-path simulation gives 16.3% and 8.1%, so the rule of thumb overstates by ~15-20%; weekly (N ~ 4) gives roughly 44%; bringing it under 5% needs N > ~300 rehedges. Transaction costs then set the practical limit (Leland 1985; Whalley and Wilmott 1997).
- Jump / gap risk: across a discontinuous move J (fraction of spot) the delta-hedged P&L is V(S(1+J),t) - V(S,t) - Delta*S*J ~= 0.5*Gamma*S^2*J^2 + (1/6)*Speed*S^3*J^3 + ..., realized all at once with no theta accrual against it. Unlike diffusion hedging error this term is not reduced by rehedging more often (there is no price to trade at inside the gap), and the Greek expansion itself breaks down for |J| larger than ~1 strike width: for the 30-day ATM example the exact convexity P&L is 0.79 per share for a 5% gap (second-order Taylor: 0.87) and 2.64 for a 10% gap (Taylor: 3.47, 30% too high). A short contract loses $79 on the 5% gap against theta income of $6.31 per trading day, i.e. 12 days of theta in one print. Long gamma profits from gaps; short gamma takes the full loss. This is why overnight and event variance are treated separately in time-weighting (below), why short-dated short-gamma positions are sized on a stress grid rather than on theta, and why the variance risk premium is partly compensation for jump risk (Bakshi and Kapadia 2003; Carr and Wu 2009).
- Why market makers "take time out" over weekends: realized variance accrues mostly during trading hours; a calendar-day clock overstates the variance expected across a weekend/holiday. Dealers price options with a time measure that weights non-trading days at a fraction of a trading day (often 0 to ~0.3 [uncertain; firm-specific, no public standard]) and may add "event variance" for scheduled announcements (earnings, FOMC, CPI). Consequence: if you compute IV with calendar time, quoted IV appears to fall on Thursday-Friday (dealers have already rolled the clock through the weekend) and rebound Monday; this "weekend vol crush" is a convention artifact whose size depends on tenor (see Practitioner heuristics), not a tradable edge. Jones and Shemesh (2018) document that option returns are abnormally low over weekends and holidays (IV is still too high relative to realized over non-trading periods); Muravyev and Ni (2020) show option returns are negative overnight and positive intraday.
- Why delta depends on the vol regime: a fixed-strike option's value is V(S, sigma_K(S)). Total delta = Delta_BS + Vega*(dsigma_K/dS). Under sticky strike, dsigma_K/dS = 0 and the BSM delta is correct (but the ATM vol drifts as spot moves across the fixed skew). Under sticky delta/moneyness with a negative skew (lower strikes carry higher IV), a rise in S moves a fixed strike "down the skew" so its IV rises: dsigma_K/dS > 0 and the call's true delta exceeds BSM. Under the local-vol/implied-tree regime (and empirically for index options most of the time), a rise in S lowers the whole vol level: dsigma_K/dS < 0 and the true call delta is below BSM (puts' delta is more negative). Order of magnitude for index options: with the 30-day ATM example (Vega 11.4 per 1.00 of vol) and a spot-vol beta of -0.5 to -1.0 vol points of 1-month ATM IV per +1% spot (a common range for the S&P 500 in calm-to-normal regimes, larger in sell-offs [uncertain; re-estimate from recent data]), the correction is -0.06 to -0.11, so the MV call delta is ~0.42-0.48 rather than 0.53: larger than the entire N(d1)-N(d2) "probability gap". The correction scales with Vega/(S*sqrt(T)) and is therefore largest for short-dated near-ATM options. Hull and White (2017) estimate dE[sigma_imp]/dS directly, as a quadratic in BSM delta, Delta_MV = Delta_BS + (Vega_BS/(S*sqrt(T)))*(a + b*Delta_BS + c*Delta_BS^2) with coefficients re-estimated on a rolling window [functional form from memory; uncertain], and find the MV delta for S&P 500 options beats the BSM delta and daily-recalibrated stochastic-vol-model deltas; the negative spot-IV correlation they document implies MV call delta below BSM. Derman (Jan. 1999) found S&P 500 behaved sticky-strike in range-bound markets, sticky-delta (moneyness) in trending markets, and sticky-implied-tree (local-vol-like) when large moves or jumps, especially downward, were expected; Daglish, Hull and Suo (2007) test trader rules of thumb against OTC S&P 500 data and argue that sticky-strike and simple sticky-delta rules are inconsistent with no-arbitrage if applied exactly [specific conclusions from training; only the abstract was verified].
- Greeks under non-BSM models: the skew-adjusted delta above is the first-order version of every model-implied delta, with dsigma_K/dS supplied by the model instead of estimated. Local vol (Dupire) produces the implied-tree regime (smile moves opposite to spot), so its call delta is below BSM under a negative skew. Models homogeneous of degree one in (S, K), such as Heston or SABR with beta = 1, produce sticky-moneyness smiles; the plain SABR delta (Hagan et al. 2002) ignores the correlated move of the vol parameter with the forward, and Bartlett (2006) adds a term proportional to Vega*rho*nu to give the "Bartlett delta", the SABR analogue of the MV delta [citation from training; not web-verified this session]. Numerical Greeks: use central differences with bumps of ~1% spot, 1 vol point and 1 day; decide whether a vega bump is sticky-strike (each strike's IV +1) or sticky-delta (the surface shifted in delta space), because the two give different vegas for OTM strikes.
- Why Greeks become unreliable near expiry: gamma, charm, speed and color are all O(1/sqrt(T)) or worse at the strike. With one hour to expiry an ATM option (S = 100, 20% IV) has gamma ~1.9 per share: a 0.50 move takes delta from ~0.5 to ~1 or ~0. A linear/quadratic Greek expansion is useless over any realistic move; the position must be analyzed as a digital-like payoff via full revaluation on a spot grid (scenario/stress analysis). The same logic applies to 0DTE index options for the whole session. Intraday theta for 0DTE is not linear in clock time: dealers decay in "variance time", with heavier weight at the open and close and around scheduled prints [magnitudes uncertain; firm-specific], so a flat per-minute theta misprices 0DTE straddles mid-session.
- Pin risk mechanics: (1) assignment uncertainty: U.S. equity-option holders decide exercise after the close; OCC's exercise-by-exception procedure exercises expiring options that are ITM by $0.01 or more per contract (equity options in customer, firm and market-maker accounts; index options in all account types) absent contrary instructions from the clearing member; OCC calls the threshold an administrative convenience for clearing members, so holders can override and brokers may apply their own cut-offs (OIC/OCC FAQ, checked 2026-10-14). A writer short an option that closes within cents of the strike does not know their Monday stock position until assignment notices arrive. SPX is European and cash-settled (no assignment of shares), but monthly AM-settled SPX carries "SET" settlement risk from the opening prints; SPXW are PM-settled. (2) Price pinning: when market makers are net long gamma in a strike with large open interest, their delta hedging sells rallies above the strike and buys dips below it, pushing spot toward the strike (Avellaneda and Lipkin 2003); Ni, Pearson and Poteshman (2005) document statistically significant clustering of optionable-stock closing prices at strikes on expiration dates. If dealers are net short gamma the flow is destabilizing (anti-pinning).
- How market makers manage inventory: they quote two-sided and absorb customer order flow, so their Greek inventory is whatever customers leave them. They (a) hedge delta nearly continuously with stock/futures (cheap, linear); (b) cannot hedge gamma/vega with the underlying, so they recycle option inventory (adjust quotes/skew to attract offsetting flow, trade with other dealers, or use listed spreads); (c) run limits on dollar gamma per 1-sigma move, vega per bucket, vanna, and on full spot x vol scenario grids; (d) price time and events explicitly; (e) widen or skew markets as inventory approaches limits. Because end users are net long index options, especially OTM puts (Garleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman 2009), and overwriting programs supply upside calls, dealers are typically short gamma/vega in downside strikes (they are short the puts customers own) and long gamma in upside strikes (they are long the calls customers sold) [the upside sidedness is less well documented and regime-dependent]. The net sign of a dealer book therefore depends on where spot sits relative to the strike distribution: net long gamma while spot is above the bulk of put open interest (stabilizing: sell rallies, buy dips) and net short once spot falls into the put strikes (destabilizing: sell into weakness); the level where the sign flips is the "gamma flip" of domain 05, and sign and magnitude vary by regime and by expiry.

## Formulas and quantitative rules
```
Notation: S spot, K strike, T years to expiry, sigma annual vol (decimal), r cont. risk-free rate,
q cont. dividend yield, phi() standard normal pdf, N() standard normal cdf.
d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r - q + 0.5*sigma^2)*T] / (sigma*sqrt(T));   d2 = d1 - sigma*sqrt(T)
Call C = S*e^(-qT)*N(d1) - K*e^(-rT)*N(d2);  Put P = K*e^(-rT)*N(-d2) - S*e^(-qT)*N(-d1)

First order
Delta_call = e^(-qT)*N(d1);          Delta_put = -e^(-qT)*N(-d1) = Delta_call - e^(-qT)
Gamma      = e^(-qT)*phi(d1) / (S*sigma*sqrt(T))                       (call = put)
Vega       = S*e^(-qT)*phi(d1)*sqrt(T)   per 1.00 of sigma; /100 for per vol point   (call = put)
Theta_call = -S*e^(-qT)*phi(d1)*sigma/(2*sqrt(T)) - r*K*e^(-rT)*N(d2) + q*S*e^(-qT)*N(d1)   per year (d/dt)
Theta_put  = -S*e^(-qT)*phi(d1)*sigma/(2*sqrt(T)) + r*K*e^(-rT)*N(-d2) - q*S*e^(-qT)*N(-d1)
Rho_call   =  K*T*e^(-rT)*N(d2);     Rho_put = -K*T*e^(-rT)*N(-d2)       per 1.00 of r
Epsilon_call (dV/dq) = -S*T*e^(-qT)*N(d1);  Epsilon_put = S*T*e^(-qT)*N(-d1)

Second order
Vanna  = dDelta/dsigma = dVega/dS = -e^(-qT)*phi(d1)*d2/sigma           (call = put)
Volga  = dVega/dsigma  = Vega*d1*d2/sigma                               (call = put)
Charm_call = dDelta/dt = q*e^(-qT)*N(d1) - e^(-qT)*phi(d1)*[2(r-q)T - d2*sigma*sqrt(T)] / (2*T*sigma*sqrt(T))

A delta-hedged risk reversal is the cleanest listed expression of a skew view.

## Empirical magnitudes
- SPX 0DTE share: same-day-expiring options were a record 66.2% of total SPX volume in July 2026 (Cboe "Market Metrics That Matter", published 2026-08-11); SPX 0DTE monthly ADV hit a record 3.4 million contracts in September 2026 and 3.2 million for the quarter, with SPX quarterly ADV of 4.9 million (Cboe, published 2026-10-12). As of 2026-10. These are volume shares, not gamma or open-interest shares. Implication: a large and intraday-only share of index dollar gamma now sits in contracts whose Greeks are the least stable; 0DTE open interest is created and extinguished within the session, so end-of-day OI snapshots miss it, which is why OI-based dealer-gamma estimates (domain 05) are stale for 0DTE [the gamma share itself is an inference; not published].
- Dealer positioning: Garleanu, Pedersen and Poteshman (2009) document that end users are net long index options, especially OTM puts, so intermediaries are net short them and index option prices carry a demand-driven premium that rises with dealers' unhedgeable risk; this is the empirical basis for the short-downside-gamma dealer picture above.
- Delta-hedged option returns are negative on average for S&P 500 index options: Bakshi and Kapadia (2003) show delta-hedged gains underperform zero, less so away from the money and more so when vol is high, consistent with a negative volatility risk premium; Coval and Shumway (2001) find zero-beta ATM straddles earn significantly negative average returns. Variance risk premium: defined as VIX (30-day implied) minus subsequent 21-trading-day realized vol, it has averaged roughly 3-5 vol points over 1990-2025 with a positive median and sign reversals in stress (Oct 2008, Feb 2018, Mar 2020) [magnitude from training; state period and estimator whenever quoted]. Carr and Wu (2009, RFS 22(3)) measure it in variance terms over 1996-2003 and find it strongly negative (realized < implied) for the S&P 500 and weaker for single names.
- Overnight vs intraday: Muravyev and Ni (2020, JFE 136(1), 219-238) find delta-hedged SPX option returns average about -1% per day close-to-open and +0.3% open-to-close; options are expensive relative to overnight realized variance and cheap relative to intraday.
- Minimum-variance delta (Hull and White 2017, J. Banking & Finance 82:180-190): empirical MV delta for S&P 500 options outperforms BSM delta and stochastic-vol-model deltas recalibrated daily; the negative spot-IV correlation implies the MV delta is below BSM for calls. Reduction in hedging-error variance is material, on the order of a fifth to a quarter for index options [uncertain; abstract gives no number; full text not fetched]. If you hedge index options with the BSM delta you are systematically over-hedged on calls and under-hedged on puts by roughly 6-11 delta points for 1-month ATM (see Mechanisms), which compounds into a short-vol-like P&L drag in sell-offs.
- Discrete hedging: daily rehedging of a one-month ATM option leaves ~16-19% of premium as P&L standard deviation (Kamal 1998: 19.3% by formula, 16.3% by simulation for an ATM put at r = 5%; the file's call example at r = 4% is not separately simulated [uncertain to within a point or two]); this is why single-option "realized vs implied" trades are noisy and why dealers rely on diversification across strikes, maturities and names.
- Pinning: Ni, Pearson and Poteshman (2005, JFE 78:49-87) find optionable stocks close at or near strikes on expiration Fridays significantly more often than on other days and attribute part of it to hedge rebalancing (and part to possible manipulation); Avellaneda and Lipkin (2003, Quant. Finance 3:417-425) derive a finite pinning probability as a function of vol, time to expiry, open interest and price impact.
- OCC exercise-by-exception threshold: $0.01 per contract ITM for expiring equity options (customer, firm and market-maker accounts) and index options (all account types), subject to contrary instructions; not literally "automatic" (OIC FAQ, as of 2026-10-14). The threshold was cut from $0.05 to $0.01 in 2008 [uncertain; date from training, OCC page not fetchable].
- Weekend effect: Jones and Shemesh (2018, JF 73(2), 861-900) report that option returns over weekends/holidays are significantly negative relative to trading-day returns (implied vol fails to fully "take out" non-trading time). As of their sample (pre-2018); whether modern dealer time-weighting has closed the gap is [uncertain].

## Practitioner heuristics
- "IV/16" daily break-even: ATM straddle needs |move| > S*IV/16 per trading day to beat theta; monthly expected move ~ S*IV*sqrt(DTE/365). For a book, report sigma_be = sqrt(-2*Theta/(Gamma*S^2)) alongside dollar gamma and theta and compare it with realized vol sampled at your hedging frequency. Breaks when realized vol is jumpy (a few large days dominate the gamma-weighted average) and when spot drifts far from the strike (gamma collapses, P&L stops accruing).
- Theta per day for ATM ~ premium/(2*DTE). For a 30-DTE ATM option worth 2.45: ~0.041/day (exact -0.044). Breaks for OTM/ITM options, which decay fastest around 2-3 months and are nearly flat (and nearly worthless) in the last two weeks unless spot approaches the strike.
- "Sell 45 DTE, close at 21 DTE" (retail theta-harvesting rule): rationale is that the gamma/theta ratio worsens inside ~3 weeks for near-ATM short premium (gamma grows as 1/sqrt(T) while remaining theta is small in dollars). The rule is about the ratio of tail risk to remaining premium, not about theta "accelerating" for OTM strikes (it does not).
- Gamma-vega mix by maturity: under ~2 weeks is a gamma trade, over ~3 months is a vega trade; 1-3 months is both. Gamma/vega = 1/(S^2*sigma*T).
- Weighted vega: assume front-month IV moves ~sqrt(T_ref/T) times the reference maturity's IV (1m moves ~1.7x the 3m vol; 1y moves ~0.5x), the one-parameter version of the mean-reversion beta in Mechanisms. Breaks in vol-term-structure inversions (crashes) where the whole curve shifts and short-dated IV can move several times more than this scaling implies.
- Regime read for delta: in grinding, range-bound tape use near-sticky-strike deltas; in trending or selling-off tape use a skew-adjusted delta (lower call delta, more negative put delta for index); re-estimate dE[sigma]/dS from recent spot-vol co-movement. Breaks when the regime shifts intraday (sticky-delta rallies can flip to local-vol sell-offs within a session).
- Hedge at the market's IV, not your forecast vol: smoother marked P&L and avoids the deep mark-to-market drawdowns that actual-vol hedging can produce before its known terminal profit arrives; put the view on through position size and strike selection.
- Rehedge by delta band, not by clock: Whalley and Wilmott (1997) show the utility-optimal no-trade band around the BSM delta has half-width (3/2*e^(-r(T-t))*k*S*Gamma^2/lambda)^(1/3): it widens with transaction cost as k^(1/3) and with gamma as Gamma^(2/3), because a high-gamma position crosses any fixed band constantly and rebalancing it to zero continuously is prohibitively expensive. A simpler desk rule: rehedge when |net delta| exceeds a fixed fraction of the delta change implied by a 1-sigma move (band proportional to Gamma*S*sigma*sqrt(dt)); fixed-interval hedging is the fallback when flows are continuous.
- Dollar-gamma budget: express gamma limits in sigma-scaled moves, not fixed percentages. A 1% move is ~1.6 daily sigma at 10 vol but ~0.5 sigma at 30 vol (1%/(IV/16)), so a fixed "P&L per 1% move" limit allows roughly (30/10)^2 = 9x more realized-move P&L in the 30-vol tape; set the limit on "P&L for a 1-sigma move" (IV/16 or realized vol/16) or rescale the percentage with the prevailing vol. Report "delta dollars after a 1-sigma move" alongside it.
- Near expiry (last 2-3 days, and all day for 0DTE): stop reading Greeks; run full revaluation on a grid of spot (e.g. +/-3% in 0.25% steps) x vol (+/-10 points) and treat near-strike shorts as digital exposure; close or roll short strikes within ~1 expected intraday move of spot to avoid pin/assignment risk.
- Hedging frequency controls diffusion noise, not gap risk: size short-gamma positions on the stress grid (what a 3-5% gap costs, in days of theta), not on theta income.
- Weekend/holiday time: count trading days (252) or weight non-trading days at a fraction. The Friday artifact in calendar-time IV scales as IV*(1 - sqrt((D-2)/D)) for D calendar days to expiry if the dealer clock drops both weekend days: negligible (<0.5 pt) beyond ~6 weeks, ~0.5-0.9 pt at 30 days, ~1-2 pts at two weeks, ~2-4 pts at one week, and 6-10 pts for an option expiring the following Monday-Tuesday (computed at 15-25 vol). Front-week "cheap Friday vol" is almost entirely this artifact; do not sell it on that basis.
- Straddle delta is not zero: the ATM-spot straddle has a small positive BSM delta (0.068 in the example) because d1 > 0 from the (r-q)T and 0.5*sigma^2*T terms; the zero-delta straddle is struck above spot (near the forward). With a negatively skewed surface the skew-adjusted straddle delta differs further.
- Spreads: compute Greeks as the signed sum of the legs and map them on a spot grid; a vertical's gamma and vega flip sign at the midpoint, a butterfly's outside the wings, so "short gamma" today can be "long gamma" after a 3% move.
- Rates/dividends: rho matters for LEAPS (1y ATM rho ~0.52 per 1% of rates at S = 100 vs 0.042 for 30d); a 1y ATM call loses ~0.62 per 1% rise in dividend yield; for American equity options, ex-date early-exercise risk on short calls is the practical dividend exposure. Assignment rule: a short ITM call is at risk on the last cum-dividend day when the dividend D exceeds the call's remaining extrinsic value, which by parity equals the same-strike put's value plus the interest on the strike, P + K*(1 - e^(-r*t_rem)); in practice check the corresponding put's bid, and if D > put bid + carry expect assignment and pre-hedge the delta jump (call delta goes to 1 and the position becomes short stock plus the dividend liability).
- Portfolio aggregation: beta-weighted delta is a first-order netting device and ignores idiosyncratic and correlation risk; index vega is not the sum of component vegas (implied correlation / dispersion); vega must be bucketed by maturity and weighted before netting; vanna and volga are netted separately because a zero-vega book can carry both.

## Common misconceptions and pitfalls
- "Delta is the probability of expiring ITM." Delta = N(d1) under BSM is the share-measure probability; N(d2) is the risk-neutral probability; neither is the real-world probability. The gap is ~0.4*sigma*sqrt(T) ATM (0.078 for 1y at 20% vol) and, as a ratio, up to ~1.4x for OTM 1y strikes.
- "Theta always accelerates into expiry." True for ATM; OTM/ITM theta peaks months before expiry and declines. Short-premium rules should separate "ATM-ish" from "far OTM" strikes. And theta is not always negative: deep-ITM European puts (r > 0) have positive theta.
- "A delta-hedged long option makes money if realized vol exceeds implied vol." Only in expectation and only in gamma-weighted terms; path dependence (where spot is when it moves) and discrete-hedging noise (std ~ 16-19% of premium with daily hedging of a 1m ATM) can swamp a several-point vol edge on a single position.
- "Hedging more often controls short-gamma risk." It shrinks diffusion hedging error as 1/sqrt(N) but does nothing for gap risk, which is the dominant loss mechanism for short-premium books.
- "Gamma-neutral implies vega-neutral (or vice versa)." Only within one maturity at one vol. Across maturities the two are independent; a long calendar is raw-vega-long and gamma-short but can be weighted-vega-short.
- "A position with zero net vega has no vol risk" is false: volga, vanna and term/skew exposure (vega by bucket) remain, and they appear in the explain as Vanna*dS*dsigma and 0.5*Volga*dsigma^2.
- "BSM delta is the right hedge ratio." It is the right ratio only under sticky-strike dynamics (or if IV of the strike truly does not co-move with spot). Index options empirically require a lower call delta / more negative put delta (Hull and White 2017), by ~6-11 delta points for 1-month ATM at typical spot-vol betas.
- "Weekend theta is free money": dealers have already taken the weekend out of time; selling Friday to buy Monday captures a convention artifact, not decay, and faces negative weekend option returns only if IV is genuinely mispriced (Jones and Shemesh 2018).
- Mixing conventions: theta per calendar day vs per trading day differs by 365/252 = 1.45x; vega per 1.00 vs per vol point by 100x; gamma per $1 vs per 1% by S/100; veta/color as d/dtau vs d/dt flip sign; a share-equivalent delta used in futures over-hedges by e^((r-q)T_F). Many "Greek" blow-ups are unit errors.
- Reading Greeks on 0DTE/near-expiry: gamma of ~1.9 per share at one hour means any Greek-based risk number is stale within minutes; use scenario P&L.
- "Spread Greeks are static": a vertical's gamma and vega change sign at the midpoint and a butterfly's outside the wings; limits expressed at today's spot are not limits after a move.
- Assuming cash-settled index options have no expiry risk: SPX monthly (AM-settled) exposes positions to the SET opening-print settlement; SPXW is PM-settled. Also AM-settled contracts stop trading the day before.
- "Dealers are always long gamma / always pin the market": sign depends on net customer positioning by strike and expiry and flips over time; the typical index book is short downside gamma and long upside gamma, so the net sign flips as spot moves; pinning requires net long dealer gamma at the strike, and anti-pinning occurs when dealers are short.

## Contested or uncertain claims
- Discrete-hedging constant: resolved this revision. sqrt(pi/4) = 0.886 is Kamal's (1998) stated prefactor and is itself an approximation that overstates the note's own simulation by ~15-20% (19.3% vs 16.3% of premium at N = 21); the constant-gamma 1/sqrt(2) = 0.71 lands closer to the simulation by accident of its cruder assumptions. Quote "roughly 16-19% for daily hedging of a 1m ATM" rather than a single constant. Both critiques of this file agreed on the attribution (sole author Michael Kamal) and the figures; the journal version's byline and pages are [uncertain].
- Which vol regime holds: Derman (1999) tied regimes to market conditions (range-bound = sticky strike, trending = sticky delta, expected jumps/sell-offs = implied tree); Daglish, Hull and Suo (2007) argue both sticky-strike and sticky-delta are internally inconsistent as exact rules and find the data favor a mix [specific findings uncertain; abstract only]; Hull and White (2017) sidestep the question with an empirical MV delta. There is no consensus on a stable rule; the practical consensus is "re-estimate the spot-vol beta frequently".
- Size of the spot-vol beta and of the MV-delta improvement: the -0.5 to -1.0 vol point per 1% figure used above is an order of magnitude from training [uncertain; regime-dependent]; Hull-White report substantial hedging gains for index options [magnitude uncertain]; some practitioners argue the gain is smaller for single stocks and during regime shifts, and that over-fitting the delta adds model risk.
- Whether dealer gamma positioning is reliably estimable from open interest (the GEX approach): proponents (SqueezeMetrics-style estimates, Barbon and Buraschi "Gamma Fragility" working paper [uncertain year]) vs skeptics who note that customer-vs-dealer sidedness is not observable in OI and that 0DTE dominance makes end-of-day OI snapshots stale. See domain 05.
- Pinning cause: hedge-rebalancing (Avellaneda-Lipkin) vs manipulation by writers; Ni-Pearson-Poteshman find evidence consistent with both. Magnitude in modern, fragmented, high-0DTE markets is [uncertain].
- Weekend-time weighting and intraday variance-time profiles: firms use proprietary weights; no public standard. Claims that "IV drops X points every Friday" are convention- and tenor-dependent (see heuristics for the arithmetic); 0DTE intraday theta profiles are [uncertain].
- Theta per calendar vs per trading day: dealers decay time in trading days, most retail platforms in calendar days; a convention difference, but it changes quoted "theta per day" by 45%.

- `05-dealer-positioning-and-option-flows`: this domain supplies the Greeks (gamma, vanna, charm) whose aggregation across dealer books generates the hedging flows modeled there; the dealer sign convention (short downside / long upside gamma, with a flip level), the pinning mechanism, and 0DTE gamma intensity are shared material.
- `04-volatility`: vol regimes (sticky strike/delta/local vol), skew-adjusted delta, vega bucketing and vol-beta weighting all depend on surface dynamics covered there; the variance risk premium (why short-gamma earns on average) is its empirical core. The path dependence of the single-option gamma trade (Gamma_i depends on S_t) is removed by holding options in proportion to 1/K^2 across strikes (the log contract; Carr and Madan 1998; Demeterfi, Derman, Kamal and Zou 1999), which has constant dollar gamma and pays exactly realized minus implied variance; `04-volatility` covers the vol-vs-variance convexity adjustment and VIX futures, `02-pricing-theory` the replication; this domain supplies the per-strike Greeks they aggregate.
- `02-pricing-theory`: the closed forms here are derivatives of that domain's pricing formula; put-call parity drives the equal-gamma/vega of calls and puts and the early-assignment test for American calls.
- `01-options-fundamentals` and `09-market-structure-regulation-and-plumbing`: OCC exercise-by-exception, AM vs PM settlement, and pin risk sit at the boundary of this domain and those (0DTE expiration mechanics have no separate file).
- `06-market-microstructure-and-intraday-behavior`: inventory limits, quote skewing and transaction costs (Leland; Whalley-Wilmott) determine rehedging frequency and the realized hedging error quantified here; fill mechanics belong to gap domain `17-options-execution-and-order-handling` (not yet written; nearest existing coverage: `09-market-structure-regulation-and-plumbing` complex-order/auction entries).
